1. ML TOPICS IMPLEMENTED SO FAR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The search is a dense-retrieval pipeline. Five ideas are actually in the code:

  a) SENTENCE EMBEDDINGS
     Each movie is converted from text into a single 384-dimensional vector of
     floats that represents its meaning, rather than its words. Texts about
     similar things land near each other in that space regardless of shared
     vocabulary. Done in embed.py by sentence-transformers all-MiniLM-L6-v2,
     a 6-layer transformer distilled from BERT. It runs locally on CPU, needs
     no API key, and caches ~90MB of weights in ~/.cache/huggingface.

  b) MEAN POOLING
     The transformer emits one vector per token, but we need one vector per
     document. MiniLM averages the token vectors together. This is the single
     most important implementation detail for understanding the model's
     behaviour, because an average is a proportion: adding tokens about
     anything else shrinks the share of the final vector devoted to any one
     concept. See finding 3 -- almost all of it follows from this.

  c) L2 NORMALIZATION
     Every vector is scaled to length exactly 1 (normalize_embeddings=True, on
     both the document side in embed.py and the query side in
     search_disney.py). This is what makes the dot product equal cosine
     similarity -- see (d). It must be applied identically on both sides or the
     scores stop meaning anything.

  d) COSINE SIMILARITY VIA DOT PRODUCT
     Similarity is the cosine of the angle between the query vector and each
     movie vector. Since a.b = |a||b|cos(theta), and normalization forced
     |a| = |b| = 1, the dot product IS the cosine. So the score is a pure
     direction comparison with magnitude divided out -- long overviews get no
     advantage from being long.

     Range is -1 to 1 in theory. In practice these embeddings occupy a narrow
     cone rather than the full sphere, so unrelated pairs score meaningfully
     above 0 and negatives are rare. Consequence: the scores are ORDINAL, not
     absolute. They rank reliably within one query but are not comparable
     across queries, and a fixed cutoff like "reject below 0.4" will not behave
     consistently. Observed noise floor varies by query -- roughly 0.27 for one
     query in finding 2, roughly 0.44 for the query in finding 3.

  e) BRUTE-FORCE k-NN SEARCH
     np.dot(db_embeddings, query_vector) scores all 826 movies in one matrix
     operation, then np.argsort picks the top k. No index, no approximation,
     exact results every time. At 826 x 384 float32 the whole matrix is ~1.3MB
     and the scan is well under a millisecond, so an ANN index like HNSW would
     add a dependency and inexact results to avoid a cost that does not exist.
     Revisit around 100k vectors.

  NOT implemented, and worth naming so the gap is explicit: no fine-tuning, no
  reranking, no hybrid keyword/BM25 scoring, and NO EVALUATION SET. Quality is
  currently judged by typing queries and eyeballing results. See section 4.

2. FINDING: THE CORPUS DESCRIBES PREMISES, NOT SCENES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The product premise is "describe a scene you remember", but TMDB overviews are
marketing blurbs describing premises, median 42 words, and 7 entries have no
overview at all (stored as "N/A").

Evidence:

  query: "a house lifted by balloons to south america"
    0.285  America's Heart & Soul
    0.271  The Haunted House
    0.270  The Three Caballeros

Up is not in the top 3 and the scores are flat and low -- that shape (flat AND
low) is what "nothing in the corpus matches" actually looks like. The Lion King
overview never mentions the stampede; Up's never mentions the balloons.

Working queries look different:

  query: "ice queen sister with magic powers"
    0.496  Frozen
    0.492  Ice Princess
    0.455  Snow White

Flat but genuinely on-topic -- the model found a real cluster.

Practical note for the UI: the GAP between the top hit and the rest is a better
"no good match" signal than the top hit's absolute value.

3. FINDING: FILLER WORDS IN THE QUERY DOMINATE THE RESULT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Big Hero 6 IS in the catalog (TMDB 177572, Walt Disney Animation Studios) and
its overview is nearly a literal restatement of the query below:

  "A special bond develops between plus-sized inflatable robot Baymax, and
   prodigy Hiro Hamada, who team up with a group of friends to form a band of
   high-tech heroes."

It still ranked 18th of 826.

  query: "A movie where there is a robot and a kid who are superheros"
    0.502  Next Avengers: Heroes of Tomorrow
    0.481  Toy Story at 20: To Infinity and Beyond
    0.470  LEGO Marvel Avengers: Mission Demolition
    0.467  A Goofy Movie
    0.438  The Incredibles
    ...
    0.371  Big Hero 6                                 <-- rank 18

Phrasing sweep, same target movie:

  rank  score  query
    18  0.371  A movie where there is a robot and a kid who are superheros
    18  0.371  A movie where there is a robot and a kid who are superheroes
     5  0.456  a robot and a kid who are superheroes
     5  0.457  robot kid superheroes
     1  0.490  inflatable robot and a boy genius

DIAGNOSIS

  The misspelling costs nothing. "superheros" and "superheroes" score
  identically to three decimals, because WordPiece tokenization splits both
  into the same subword pieces. Ruled out.

  The filler costs 13 ranks. Deleting "A movie where there is" moves Big Hero 6
  from 18 to 5 with no other change.

  This is mean pooling, item (b). Seven of ~13 tokens in the original query are
  grammatical scaffolding: a, movie, where, there, is, who, are. Roughly half
  the query vector therefore encodes "generic English sentence about a movie",
  which points at the dense center of the embedding space where everything is
  mildly similar to everything. Only the remaining half encodes
  robot/kid/superhero. Cosine measures direction, so halving the concept's
  share of the direction is a real penalty.

  The same dilution hurts Big Hero 6 on the document side. Its overview spends
  most of its length on proper nouns and texture -- "Baymax", "Hiro Hamada",
  "plus-sized inflatable", "a special bond develops" -- which carry almost no
  transferable meaning. Next Avengers: Heroes of Tomorrow is short and is about
  nothing except hero children, so a large fraction of its vector points
  exactly where the query points.

  A Goofy Movie at 0.467, which has nothing to do with robots or superheroes,
  establishes the noise floor for this query at roughly 0.44. Big Hero 6 at
  0.371 scored BELOW the noise floor -- the model extracted essentially no
  usable signal from its overview.

GENERAL RULE

  Cosine over mean-pooled embeddings rewards the PROPORTION of a text devoted
  to a concept, not whether the concept is present. A short document entirely
  about X beats a longer document that mentions X alongside other things. The
  search is not finding what CONTAINS the concept, it is finding what IS MOSTLY
  the concept. This applies to both sides: query and document.

4. PROPOSED FIX
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Three changes, in the order I would do them. The first is one line, the second
is the real fix, the third is what tells you whether either worked.

--- 4a. SWAP THE MODEL (one line, do this first) ------------------------------

  all-MiniLM-L6-v2 is trained for SYMMETRIC similarity: sentence vs sentence,
  where both sides are the same kind of text and roughly the same length. Its
  training objective was "are these two sentences paraphrases".

  This project is ASYMMETRIC retrieval: a short user query vs a longer
  descriptive passage. Different shape, different objective. multi-qa-MiniLM-
  L6-cos-v1 is trained on ~215M question/answer pairs specifically for that
  shape, using a contrastive objective -- pull the true query/passage pair
  together, push apart the other passages in the batch (in-batch negatives).
  That training is precisely what teaches a model to ignore the query-side
  filler in finding 3, because "A movie where there is..." appeared in its
  training queries too and carried no signal about which passage was correct.

  It is a drop-in swap: same 6-layer architecture, same 384 dims, same speed,
  same mean pooling, same cosine normalization. Nothing downstream changes.

    embed.py:9          EMBEDDING_MODEL = 'multi-qa-MiniLM-L6-cos-v1'
    search_disney.py:16 EMBEDDING_MODEL = "multi-qa-MiniLM-L6-cos-v1"

  Then re-run embed.py. NOTE: the constant is duplicated in two files and the
  two MUST match -- encoding the query with a different model than the
  documents produces garbage silently, no error. Worth importing the constant
  from one place rather than keeping two copies in sync by hand.

--- 4b. ENRICH THE EMBEDDED TEXT (the real fix) -------------------------------

  Finding 3's general rule says concept proportion is what matters, so raise
  the concept density of each document. TMDB /movie/{id}/keywords is the cheap
  source: for Big Hero 6 it returns terms like "robot", "superhero", "brother
  brother relationship", "san fransokyo". Pure concept, zero filler, and it is
  the vocabulary users actually type.

  This also directly attacks finding 2. Keywords are frequently scene-level and
  thematic where overviews are premise-level -- this is the documented path to
  making "describe a scene you remember" work at all.

  Change in embed.py, roughly:

    text_chunk = (f"Title: {title}, Overview: {overview}, "
                  f"Keywords: {', '.join(keywords)}")

  Cost: one extra TMDB call per movie in build_catalog.py (826 calls, a few
  minutes, cache them into catalog.json so embed.py stays offline), then
  re-run embed.py. Taglines are a second cheap source in the same request.

  CAUTION: keywords lengthen the document, and by the mean-pooling logic that
  dilutes the overview's contribution. The expected win is that keywords are
  denser in useful signal than the text they dilute -- likely but NOT
  guaranteed. This is exactly why 4c exists.

--- 4c. BUILD A SMALL EVALUATION SET (do this before or alongside 4b) ---------

  Right now quality is judged by typing a query and eyeballing the output.
  That cannot tell you whether a change helped overall or just helped the one
  query you happened to look at -- and 4a and 4b will each help some queries
  and hurt others. Without a measurement, "did that work" is unanswerable.

  Minimum viable version: a JSON file of 30-50 hand-written pairs of
  {query, expected TMDB id}, written the way a real user would type -- filler
  and misspellings included, since that is the actual input distribution.
  Draw them from movies you know well.

  Then score two numbers:
    Recall@5  -- fraction of queries where the right movie is in the top 5.
                 This is the one users feel.
    MRR       -- mean of 1/rank of the correct movie. Rewards moving a film
                 from rank 5 to rank 1, which Recall@5 cannot see.

  Record the baseline BEFORE changing anything, then re-measure after 4a and
  after 4b. Keep the change only if the numbers move. Big Hero 6 for the
  finding-3 query is one row of this set: currently rank 18, MRR contribution
  0.056, Recall@5 miss.

  This is the difference between tuning and guessing, and it is the piece the
  project is missing most.
