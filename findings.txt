1. ML TOPICS IMPLEMENTED SO FAR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The search is a dense-retrieval pipeline. Five ideas are actually in the code:

  a) SENTENCE EMBEDDINGS
     Each movie is converted from text into a single 384-dimensional vector of
     floats that represents its meaning, rather than its words. Texts about
     similar things land near each other in that space regardless of shared
     vocabulary. Done in embed.py by sentence-transformers all-MiniLM-L6-v2,
     a 6-layer transformer distilled from BERT. It runs locally on CPU, needs
     no API key, and caches ~90MB of weights in ~/.cache/huggingface.

  b) MEAN POOLING
     The transformer emits one vector per token, but we need one vector per
     document. MiniLM averages the token vectors together. This is the single
     most important implementation detail for understanding the model's
     behaviour, because an average is a proportion: adding tokens about
     anything else shrinks the share of the final vector devoted to any one
     concept. See finding 3 -- almost all of it follows from this.

  c) L2 NORMALIZATION
     Every vector is scaled to length exactly 1 (normalize_embeddings=True, on
     both the document side in embed.py and the query side in
     search_disney.py). This is what makes the dot product equal cosine
     similarity -- see (d). It must be applied identically on both sides or the
     scores stop meaning anything.

  d) COSINE SIMILARITY VIA DOT PRODUCT
     Similarity is the cosine of the angle between the query vector and each
     movie vector. Since a.b = |a||b|cos(theta), and normalization forced
     |a| = |b| = 1, the dot product IS the cosine. So the score is a pure
     direction comparison with magnitude divided out -- long overviews get no
     advantage from being long.

     Range is -1 to 1 in theory. In practice these embeddings occupy a narrow
     cone rather than the full sphere, so unrelated pairs score meaningfully
     above 0 and negatives are rare. Consequence: the scores are ORDINAL, not
     absolute. They rank reliably within one query but are not comparable
     across queries, and a fixed cutoff like "reject below 0.4" will not behave
     consistently. Observed noise floor varies by query -- roughly 0.27 for one
     query in finding 2, roughly 0.44 for the query in finding 3.

  e) BRUTE-FORCE k-NN SEARCH
     np.dot(db_embeddings, query_vector) scores all 826 movies in one matrix
     operation, then np.argsort picks the top k. No index, no approximation,
     exact results every time. At 826 x 384 float32 the whole matrix is ~1.3MB
     and the scan is well under a millisecond, so an ANN index like HNSW would
     add a dependency and inexact results to avoid a cost that does not exist.
     Revisit around 100k vectors.

  NOT implemented, and worth naming so the gap is explicit: no fine-tuning, no
  reranking, no hybrid keyword/BM25 scoring, and NO EVALUATION SET. Quality is
  currently judged by typing queries and eyeballing results. See section 4.

2. FINDING: THE CORPUS DESCRIBES PREMISES, NOT SCENES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The product premise is "describe a scene you remember", but TMDB overviews are
marketing blurbs describing premises, median 42 words, and 7 entries have no
overview at all (stored as "N/A").

Evidence:

  query: "a house lifted by balloons to south america"
    0.285  America's Heart & Soul
    0.271  The Haunted House
    0.270  The Three Caballeros

Up is not in the top 3 and the scores are flat and low -- that shape (flat AND
low) is what "nothing in the corpus matches" actually looks like. The Lion King
overview never mentions the stampede; Up's never mentions the balloons.

Working queries look different:

  query: "ice queen sister with magic powers"
    0.496  Frozen
    0.492  Ice Princess
    0.455  Snow White

Flat but genuinely on-topic -- the model found a real cluster.

Practical note for the UI: the GAP between the top hit and the rest is a better
"no good match" signal than the top hit's absolute value.

3. FINDING: FILLER WORDS IN THE QUERY DOMINATE THE RESULT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Big Hero 6 IS in the catalog (TMDB 177572, Walt Disney Animation Studios) and
its overview is nearly a literal restatement of the query below:

  "A special bond develops between plus-sized inflatable robot Baymax, and
   prodigy Hiro Hamada, who team up with a group of friends to form a band of
   high-tech heroes."

It still ranked 18th of 826.

  query: "A movie where there is a robot and a kid who are superheros"
    0.502  Next Avengers: Heroes of Tomorrow
    0.481  Toy Story at 20: To Infinity and Beyond
    0.470  LEGO Marvel Avengers: Mission Demolition
    0.467  A Goofy Movie
    0.438  The Incredibles
    ...
    0.371  Big Hero 6                                 <-- rank 18

Phrasing sweep, same target movie:

  rank  score  query
    18  0.371  A movie where there is a robot and a kid who are superheros
    18  0.371  A movie where there is a robot and a kid who are superheroes
     5  0.456  a robot and a kid who are superheroes
     5  0.457  robot kid superheroes
     1  0.490  inflatable robot and a boy genius

DIAGNOSIS

  The misspelling costs nothing. "superheros" and "superheroes" score
  identically to three decimals, because WordPiece tokenization splits both
  into the same subword pieces. Ruled out.

  The filler costs 13 ranks. Deleting "A movie where there is" moves Big Hero 6
  from 18 to 5 with no other change.

  This is mean pooling, item (b). Seven of ~13 tokens in the original query are
  grammatical scaffolding: a, movie, where, there, is, who, are. Roughly half
  the query vector therefore encodes "generic English sentence about a movie",
  which points at the dense center of the embedding space where everything is
  mildly similar to everything. Only the remaining half encodes
  robot/kid/superhero. Cosine measures direction, so halving the concept's
  share of the direction is a real penalty.

  The same dilution hurts Big Hero 6 on the document side. Its overview spends
  most of its length on proper nouns and texture -- "Baymax", "Hiro Hamada",
  "plus-sized inflatable", "a special bond develops" -- which carry almost no
  transferable meaning. Next Avengers: Heroes of Tomorrow is short and is about
  nothing except hero children, so a large fraction of its vector points
  exactly where the query points.

  A Goofy Movie at 0.467, which has nothing to do with robots or superheroes,
  establishes the noise floor for this query at roughly 0.44. Big Hero 6 at
  0.371 scored BELOW the noise floor -- the model extracted essentially no
  usable signal from its overview.

GENERAL RULE

  Cosine over mean-pooled embeddings rewards the PROPORTION of a text devoted
  to a concept, not whether the concept is present. A short document entirely
  about X beats a longer document that mentions X alongside other things. The
  search is not finding what CONTAINS the concept, it is finding what IS MOSTLY
  the concept. This applies to both sides: query and document.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. PROPOSED FIX
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Three changes, in the order I would do them. The first is one line, the second
is the real fix, the third is what tells you whether either worked.

--- 4a. SWAP THE MODEL (one line, do this first) ------------------------------

  all-MiniLM-L6-v2 is trained for SYMMETRIC similarity: sentence vs sentence,
  where both sides are the same kind of text and roughly the same length. Its
  training objective was "are these two sentences paraphrases".

  This project is ASYMMETRIC retrieval: a short user query vs a longer
  descriptive passage. Different shape, different objective. multi-qa-MiniLM-
  L6-cos-v1 is trained on ~215M question/answer pairs specifically for that
  shape, using a contrastive objective -- pull the true query/passage pair
  together, push apart the other passages in the batch (in-batch negatives).
  That training is precisely what teaches a model to ignore the query-side
  filler in finding 3, because "A movie where there is..." appeared in its
  training queries too and carried no signal about which passage was correct.

  It is a drop-in swap: same 6-layer architecture, same 384 dims, same speed,
  same mean pooling, same cosine normalization. Nothing downstream changes.

    embed.py:9          EMBEDDING_MODEL = 'multi-qa-MiniLM-L6-cos-v1'
    search_disney.py:16 EMBEDDING_MODEL = "multi-qa-MiniLM-L6-cos-v1"

  Then re-run embed.py. NOTE: the constant is duplicated in two files and the
  two MUST match -- encoding the query with a different model than the
  documents produces garbage silently, no error. Worth importing the constant
  from one place rather than keeping two copies in sync by hand.

--- 4b. ENRICH THE EMBEDDED TEXT (the real fix) -------------------------------

  Finding 3's general rule says concept proportion is what matters, so raise
  the concept density of each document. TMDB /movie/{id}/keywords is the cheap
  source: for Big Hero 6 it returns terms like "robot", "superhero", "brother
  brother relationship", "san fransokyo". Pure concept, zero filler, and it is
  the vocabulary users actually type.

  This also directly attacks finding 2. Keywords are frequently scene-level and
  thematic where overviews are premise-level -- this is the documented path to
  making "describe a scene you remember" work at all.

  Change in embed.py, roughly:

    text_chunk = (f"Title: {title}, Overview: {overview}, "
                  f"Keywords: {', '.join(keywords)}")

  Cost: one extra TMDB call per movie in build_catalog.py (826 calls, a few
  minutes, cache them into catalog.json so embed.py stays offline), then
  re-run embed.py. Taglines are a second cheap source in the same request.

  CAUTION: keywords lengthen the document, and by the mean-pooling logic that
  dilutes the overview's contribution. The expected win is that keywords are
  denser in useful signal than the text they dilute -- likely but NOT
  guaranteed. This is exactly why 4c exists.

--- 4c. BUILD A SMALL EVALUATION SET (do this before or alongside 4b) ---------

  Right now quality is judged by typing a query and eyeballing the output.
  That cannot tell you whether a change helped overall or just helped the one
  query you happened to look at -- and 4a and 4b will each help some queries
  and hurt others. Without a measurement, "did that work" is unanswerable.

  Minimum viable version: a JSON file of 30-50 hand-written pairs of
  {query, expected TMDB id}, written the way a real user would type -- filler
  and misspellings included, since that is the actual input distribution.
  Draw them from movies you know well.

  Then score two numbers:
    Recall@5  -- fraction of queries where the right movie is in the top 5.
                 This is the one users feel.
    MRR       -- mean of 1/rank of the correct movie. Rewards moving a film
                 from rank 5 to rank 1, which Recall@5 cannot see.

  Record the baseline BEFORE changing anything, then re-measure after 4a and
  after 4b. Keep the change only if the numbers move. Big Hero 6 for the
  finding-3 query is one row of this set: currently rank 18, MRR contribution
  0.056, Recall@5 miss.

  This is the difference between tuning and guessing, and it is the piece the
  project is missing most.


2026-09-16 -- TMDB keyword/tagline enrichment
------------------------------------------------------------
Cached keywords/taglines for all 826 existing records without changing original
fields or ordering. 692 have keywords; 521 have taglines. Separate overview and
extra-text passages produce 1546 normalized vectors. Max passage score selects
unique movie candidates; the cross-encoder receives all fields together.
Same 50 premise queries and models, candidate_k=50:
  Top 1: 35/50 -> 41/50; Recall@5: 42/50 -> 48/50.
  MRR: 0.762 -> 0.878; stage-1 Recall@50: 47/50 -> 49/50.
No final-rank regressions on this set. Remaining misses: Guardians (bi 29,
rerank 20) and Star Wars (bi 64, outside candidates). Scene queries not evaluated.
Original evaluation/baseline.json and evaluation/results.csv are unchanged.
New outputs: evaluation/enriched.json, enriched.csv, enrichment_comparison.md.
These are development-set results, not a held-out estimate.

Enrichment source and scoring clarification:
Keywords and taglines are fetched from actual TMDB movie entries, not generated.
documents.py defines the shared text formatting. Stage 1 takes the maximum of
the overview and keywords/tagline passage scores per movie (not an average).
Stage 2 scores the query against one combined title/overview/keywords/tagline
passage, with no hand-assigned field weights. Stage-1 scores select candidates
but are not blended into the final cross-encoder scores. The measured gain is
for both stages together; it does not isolate either stage or either new field.
See README.md, "Where enrichment comes from and how it is weighted".


2026-09-17 -- First cross-encoder fine-tune, experimental
------------------------------------------------------------
Fine-tuned cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2 on the agent-authored trial
set (evaluation/synthetic_seed.json, 377 queries / 277 movies, three
collection batches; database/agent_feedback.sqlite3). Human review of the
outcome=other rows (correct film missing from the top 5 entirely) turned up
nothing worth discarding. Split 277 movies 70/15/15 into train/val/test
(evaluation/build_split.py, seeded, stratified by group size and outcome so a
movie's premise+scene pair never crosses a split and the "other" rows aren't
concentrated in one split by chance) -- 194/42/41 movies, 264/57/56 queries.

Training data: one triplet per training query (query, confirmed movie's
document text, hardest wrong top-5 candidate by current cross-encoder score)
via document_text() from documents.py -- exactly the text stage 2 already
scores in production, so the fine-tune trains on the same text shape it will
serve. Objective: MultipleNegativesRankingLoss (pairwise/contrastive), 4
epochs, lr 2e-5, sentence-transformers' CrossEncoderTrainer. Checkpoint
selection used CrossEncoderRerankingEvaluator (MRR@5/NDCG@5) on the val split,
not training loss. evaluation/finetune_cross_encoder.py does all of this and
writes evaluation/finetune_results.json.

Held-out test split (56 queries the model never trained on, n too small for
tight confidence intervals but consistent with the val split's direction):
  MRR@5:  base bi-encoder order 0.629 -> current production reranker 0.704
          -> fine-tuned reranker 0.753
  NDCG@5: base 0.673 -> current production 0.743 -> fine-tuned 0.793
Val split moved the same direction (MRR@5 0.699 -> 0.731, NDCG@5 0.732 ->
0.755), so the test-split gain is not just favorable checkpoint selection on
val leaking into val's own number.

Dev-set regression check (evaluation/eval_checkpoint.py against the original
hand-written queries.json, which this whole synthetic pipeline never touches):
Recall@5 held exactly at 48/50 (96%), MRR ticked up 0.878 -> 0.885. No
regression on the set that mattered most before any of this started.

Caveats this is not: not a held-out estimate at production scale (56 test
queries from one agent's memory, not real users); the agent-authored ground
truth labels are still self-reported, only spot-checked, not independently
verified; MultipleNegativesRankingLoss further loosens the cross-encoder
logit's already-uncalibrated relationship to the sigmoid percentage (see
"Score display" in CLAUDE.md), so display calibration remains completely
separate, unaddressed work. The checkpoint lives at
models/cross-encoder-finetuned-v1/final (gitignored) and is NOT wired into the
backend -- CROSS_ENCODER_MODEL in disney_cross_encode.py still points at the
pretrained model. Swapping it in is a separate, deliberate decision.

Per-query detail, all three splits, both models, every one of the 377 queries:
evaluation/compare_checkpoints.py writes evaluation/finetune_comparison.csv
(full table) and evaluation/finetune_comparison.md (aggregate table + biggest
rank changes + regressions). It scores each query over only the 5 candidates
already stored from collection time, so for outcome=other queries -- where the
confirmed movie isn't one of those 5 by definition -- it can only say "not
among these 5," not distinguish why. That comparison originally reported "77
queries neither model ever saw," which conflates two different causes and is
superseded by the breakdown below. Of the 300 queries whose confirmed movie
WAS one of the stored 5: 35 improved rank, 4 regressed (McFarland USA, Finding
Dory, Thor: Ragnarok, Tarzan -- all off by one or two ranks, none dropped out
of the top 5 entirely), the rest unchanged. evaluation/eval_train_split.py
adds the train-split numbers to finetune_results.json for the same reason --
train's gain (+0.057 MRR@5) is the same order of magnitude as val (+0.032) and
test (+0.049), which is evidence against gross overfitting, not proof against
it.

2026-09-17 -- Stage-1 ceiling vs. stage-2 demotion, properly separated
------------------------------------------------------------
The "77" above only checked each query's previously-stored top-5 candidates,
not the full CANDIDATE_K=50 bi-encoder pool -- so it couldn't tell a true
stage-1 miss (confirmed movie beyond the bi-encoder's top 50, unreachable by
any reranker) apart from a stage-2 demotion (movie WAS in the 50, but got
pushed below rank 5 by the reranker, which a better reranker could in
principle fix). evaluation/diagnose_stage1_ceiling.py re-retrieves the actual
50-candidate pool fresh per query (disney_overview_search/search_disney.py's
search_movies(), same call the backend makes) and rescores all 50 with both
the production and fine-tuned checkpoints, writing evaluation/stage1_ceiling.csv.

Real breakdown, all 377 queries:
  hit before and after:     299  (already worked)
  stage-1 ceiling miss:      36  (never in the bi-encoder's 50; no reranker
                                  fine-tune, v1 or any future one, can fix
                                  these -- needs a better bi-encoder or richer
                                  corpus text)
  stage-2, still missed:     33  (in the 50, both rerankers still miss top 5)
  stage-2, fixed:             8  (in the 50, production missed top 5,
                                  fine-tuned v1 now hits it)
  stage-2, regressed:         1  (production hit, fine-tuned v1 now misses)

So only 36 of the original 77 "other" outcomes are genuinely unfixable by
cross-encoder work -- the other 41 were always fair game, and v1 already
recovered 8 of them without deliberately targeting this failure mode.

Of the 33 still-missed stage-2 cases, 22 are in the TRAIN split -- v1 had
these exact queries available during training and still didn't learn to rank
them correctly. That points at a specific, fixable weakness in how v1 mined
negatives, not an inherent limit: build_train_dataset() in
finetune_cross_encoder.py drew its one hard negative per query only from the
previously-stored top-5 (at most 4 wrong candidates), never from the other
~46 movies in the actual 50-candidate pool. The model was trained to beat
whichever 4 wrong movies happened to survive the ORIGINAL reranking, not the
truly hardest, most-confusable candidates. Planned v2 fix: rebuild training
triplets from the full 50-candidate pool per query and mine multiple (3-4)
hard negatives per query instead of one, using the same
MultipleNegativesRankingLoss n-tuple support already available. splits.json
stays untouched so v2's numbers remain comparable to v1's on the same
held-out queries.


2026-09-18 -- v2 negative-mining sweep: more negatives did not help
------------------------------------------------------------
Tested the v2 hypothesis from above. evaluation/build_candidate_pool.py caches
the full CANDIDATE_K=50 pool + production cross-encoder score per query, once
(evaluation/full_candidate_pool.json), so training-negative mining and val/test
evaluation both draw from the actual 50 candidates rather than each query's
old stored top-5 -- this also fixes a v1 evaluation gap: reranking only a
pre-curated 5 is an easier task than the real 50-candidate one the backend
poses. evaluation/finetune_v2.py trains a config for a given --num-negatives
and evaluates val/test on this fair full-50 basis.

Three-way comparison, full-50 evaluation, all measuring the identical val/test
queries:
  config           val MRR@5 / NDCG@5      test MRR@5 / NDCG@5
  production       0.699 / 0.732           0.704 / 0.743
  v1 (1 neg, old top-5 pool)   0.731 / 0.755   0.749 / 0.786
  neg_1 (1 neg, full 50 pool)  0.731 / 0.755   0.749 / 0.786   -- IDENTICAL to v1
  neg_4 (4 negs, full 50 pool) 0.725 / 0.751   0.737 / 0.777   -- worse than both

v1 and neg_1 score identically to three decimal places (evaluation/
refit_v1_full50.py re-scored v1's actual checkpoint on the same full-50 basis
to make this comparison fair -- no retraining needed since v1's checkpoint is
fixed). Reading: the single *hardest* wrong candidate per query -- the one
the current model is most confused about -- is almost always already sitting
in the top 5 anyway, since being "hardest" and being highly-scored are nearly
the same thing. Widening the mining pool from 5 to 50 essentially never
changed which one negative got picked, so v1 and neg_1's training data (and
resulting checkpoints) ended up equivalent.

neg_4 (more negatives per query, still from the full 50) did WORSE than
either 1-negative config, not better, despite 22 of 33 stage-2 "still missed"
train-split queries having been directly available during v1's training. So
the lever that mattered was not "search deeper for one hard negative" (no
effect) or "use more explicit hard negatives" (net negative at n=4 on 264
training queries) -- something else is the bottleneck: more epochs, a
different loss (MarginMSE or a listwise loss), semi-hard rather than
hardest-possible mining (avoiding accidentally-plausible alternates), or
simply more/better training data. Not yet tested. neg_8 was planned but
skipped once neg_4 already regressed -- unlikely to reverse the trend.

Caught and fixed one bug along the way: CrossEncoderRerankingEvaluator.
primary_metric is "ndcg@5" (no name prefix) until the evaluator has actually
been __call__'d once, at which point it mutates to "val_ndcg@5" as a side
effect. finetune_v2.py originally read val_evaluator.primary_metric to set
metric_for_best_model before the evaluator's first call on a cached-baseline
run, which crashed with a KeyError the first time the shared before-baseline
was reused instead of recomputed. Fixed by hardcoding the real key
(f"val_ndcg@{TOP_K}") instead of trusting the mutable attribute.

Follow-up: how many of the 36 v1-era stage-1 ceiling misses (findings.txt's
first 2026-09-17 entry) are just outside CANDIDATE_K=50 versus genuinely far
away? evaluation/stage1_ceiling_depth.py re-retrieves each with no cutoff:
  raise CANDIDATE_K to 75   ->  6/36 back in reach
  raise CANDIDATE_K to 100  -> 14/36 back in reach
  raise CANDIDATE_K to 150  -> 17/36 back in reach
  raise CANDIDATE_K to 200  -> 23/36 back in reach
Steep diminishing returns; some (Ralph Breaks the Internet at true rank 557,
Star Wars Episode I at 539) are nowhere close and need a real bi-encoder fix,
not a bigger cutoff.

Measured whether raising CANDIDATE_K to 100 actually helps, rather than
trusting "in reach" as a proxy: evaluation/eval_candidate_k100.py reruns
retrieval+reranking (v1 checkpoint, which ties neg_1) against a fresh
100-candidate pool (evaluation/full_candidate_pool_k100.json) for all 377
queries. Result: ceiling misses dropped 36 -> 22 as predicted, but only 5 of
the 14 newly-in-pool queries actually reached the top 5 -- the other 9 are
technically reachable now but still buried below rank 5, because more
candidates in the pool means more distractors for the reranker too, not a
free improvement. One regression: a query the fine-tuned model correctly hit
at K=50 now misses at K=100, because a candidate from the newly-added 51-100
range outscored the true movie. Net: +5 hits, -1 regression, out of 377
queries, at roughly double the reranking cost per query (~0.15s -> ~0.3s at
this corpus size). Not a clear win to ship on its own.

Where this leaves things: the 22 movies still unreachable even at
CANDIDATE_K=100 need bi-encoder-level work (a different embedding model, or
fine-tuning the bi-encoder itself) -- no amount of reranker tuning or
candidate-cutoff raising touches them, since they never reach the reranker.
Nothing from this session is deployed: CROSS_ENCODER_MODEL and CANDIDATE_K in
disney_cross_encode.py are unchanged from before this investigation started.
